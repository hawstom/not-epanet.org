What EPANET actually is

Written by people who use it every day and have no connection to the agency that made it. If anything below is wrong, EPA's own page is the authority and this one is not.

Once more, plainly

This page is about EPANET. It is not by EPANET, it is not by the EPA, and nothing on it is approved by either. EPANET lives at epa.gov/water-research/epanet, and its continuing open development is at github.com/OpenWaterAnalytics/EPANET.

The program

A public-domain model of a pipe network

EPANET performs extended-period simulation of the hydraulic and water-quality behaviour of pressurised pipe networks. You describe a system of junctions, reservoirs, tanks, pipes, pumps, and valves; it computes the pressure at every junction, the flow in every pipe, the level in every tank, and the fate of a chemical or of the water's own age as it travels. It runs an instant or a week.

It was created by Lewis A. Rossman for the United States Environmental Protection Agency's water research programme, and first appeared in 1993, with a formal report on the EPANET water quality model published the same year. EPANET 2 followed. Version 2.2 was the last release made by EPA itself.

It is used by utilities, consulting engineers, regulators, and universities all over the world, and its computational engine is embedded in a large number of other programs, commercial and free, this one included. Describing it as the industry reference for network hydraulics is not marketing language; it is simply where the field ended up.

Why it costs nothing, and what that means

EPANET is not free because somebody decided to be generous with a price. It is free because United States copyright law does not permit a work of the federal government to be copyrighted at all. 17 U.S.C. § 105 says copyright protection is not available for any work of the United States government, and a work of the United States government is defined as one prepared by an officer or employee as part of that person's official duties.

The practical consequences are worth spelling out, because they are unusual:

  • There is no licence to accept. Public domain is not a licence. There is nothing to agree to and no terms to comply with.
  • There is no owner to ask. Nobody can grant or withhold permission, because nobody holds the right that permission would come from.
  • Anybody may do anything, including take it private. A company may build EPANET into a product and sell it, and need not publish what it changed. Many have. That is not a loophole; it is the deliberate design of the public domain.
  • It cannot be withdrawn. No future decision by anybody can make the existing code less available than it is today.

Our own project made the opposite choice, GNU GPL v3 or later, and is honest on the front page about that being a restriction EPA did not impose.

Who develops it now

After EPA's 2.2, EPANET's development continued in the open at Open Water Analytics, a community effort in collaboration with EPA. Version 2.3 was released on 2024-07-17 and 2.3.5 on 2025-02-20. The engine our network editor runs is OWA-EPANET 2.3.5, which is why a run report from our page prints a version number higher than the one on EPA's download page.

If you want EPANET, get EPANET

This site exists to point you at something else, so it should be clear about when the something else is the wrong answer. Use EPANET itself if you need the reference implementation, if you need a feature we do not have, if your reviewer expects EPANET output, or if you simply prefer it. It is free, it is complete, it is maintained, and it does not need our permission or our approval any more than we need its.
